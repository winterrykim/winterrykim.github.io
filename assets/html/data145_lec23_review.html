Bridge: From Jacobians to Gaussian Geometry

Lecture 22 emphasized affine transformations, Jacobians, and the fact that multivariate normals behave cleanly under linear maps. Lecture 23 takes that machinery and turns it into statistics: instead of changing variables to compute a density, we change coordinates to separate signal directions from residual directions.

The point is not that the data themselves change. We are looking at the same Gaussian cloud from a basis that makes the testing problem easy to read off.

Once the coordinates are chosen well, the classical families line up: $\chi^2$ is a squared Gaussian length, $t$ is one signal coordinate divided by estimated noise, and $F$ is one variance estimate divided by another. The whole lecture is a geometric unification of tests you already know.

Lecture 23: Probability Review and Rotational Invariance

23.1 Why \(\chi^2\), \(t\), and \(F\) show up together

If $Z_1, \ldots, Z_d \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(0,1)$, then $$V = \sum_{j=1}^d Z_j^2 \sim \chi^2_d.$$ This is the total squared length of a standard Gaussian vector in $d$ dimensions.
If $Z \sim N(0,1)$ and $V \sim \chi^2_d$ are independent, then $$T = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/d}} \sim t_d.$$ Here the numerator is a standardized signal coordinate and $V/d$ is an average squared noise level per degree of freedom.
If $V_1 \sim \chi^2_{d_1}$ and $V_2 \sim \chi^2_{d_2}$ are independent, then $$F = \frac{V_1/d_1}{V_2/d_2} \sim F_{d_1,d_2}.$$ So $F$ compares two variance estimates built from orthogonal Gaussian pieces.
Shortest conceptual summary: $$\chi^2 = \text{squared Gaussian length}, \qquad t = \frac{\text{signal}}{\text{estimated noise}}, \qquad F = \frac{\text{variance estimate}}{\text{variance estimate}}.$$

23.2 What the degrees of freedom \(d\) means

If $V \sim \chi^2_d$, then by definition $$V = U_1^2 + \cdots + U_d^2$$ for $d$ independent $N(0,1)$ variables. So the number $d$ is not an arbitrary adjustment: it is the number of independent Gaussian directions being squared.

$$\E[V] = d \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \E[V/d] = 1.$$
Dimension of a Gaussian subspace = degrees of freedom of the chi-squared built from that subspace. Dividing by $d$ turns total squared variation into average squared variation per direction.

23.3 Rotational invariance and projection lengths

Affine transformation formula. If $Z \sim N_n(\mu,\Sigma)$ and $Y = AZ + b$, then $$Y \sim N(A\mu + b,\; A \Sigma A^T).$$

Apply this when $\mu = 0$, $\Sigma = \sigma^2 I_n$, $b = 0$, and $A = Q$ is orthogonal. Then $$QZ \sim N_n(0,\sigma^2 QQ^T) = N_n(0,\sigma^2 I_n),$$ so the distribution is unchanged by rotation or reflection.

If $Z \sim N_n(0,\sigma^2 I_n)$ and $V_1, V_2$ are orthogonal subspaces with dimensions $d_1$ and $d_2$, then $$\|P_{V_1}Z\|^2 \sim \sigma^2 \chi^2_{d_1}, \qquad \|P_{V_2}Z\|^2 \sim \sigma^2 \chi^2_{d_2},$$ and these two squared projection lengths are independent.
Choose an orthonormal basis adapted to $V_1$, $V_2$, and the remaining orthogonal complement. In that rotated basis, the coordinates are iid $N(0,\sigma^2)$, and the projections are just sums of squares of disjoint coordinate blocks.
The chi-squared does not appear just because something is centered at 0. It appears because we take a sum of squares of standardized Gaussian coordinates.

Canonical Linear Model: The \(n=2\) Prototype

23.4 Setup

Start with the simplest canonical model: $$Z \sim N_2 \left(\binom{\mu_1}{0},\sigma^2 I_2 \right), \qquad H_0:\mu_1=0 \text{ vs } H_1:\mu_1 \neq 0.$$ Only $Z_1$ contains signal. The second coordinate $Z_2$ has mean $0$ under both hypotheses, so it is pure residual noise.

Under $H_1$, the mean shifts to the right. The vertical coordinate $Z_2$ does not change in distribution, but the observation still becomes more aligned with the horizontal signal axis because $Z_1$ tends to be larger relative to $Z_2$.

23.5 Known \(\sigma^2\): \(z\)-test and \(\chi^2_1\)-test

If $\sigma^2$ is known, the natural standardized statistic is $$\frac{Z_1}{\sigma} \sim N(0,1) \qquad \text{under } H_0.$$ A two-sided test rejects for large $|Z_1|/\sigma$.

Squaring gives $$\left(\frac{Z_1}{\sigma}\right)^2 \sim \chi^2_1.$$ So the two-sided $z$-test and the $\chi^2_1$-test are exactly the same rejection rule written two ways.

One-sided alternatives are different: the signed $z$-statistic remembers whether the shift is left or right, while the squared $\chi^2_1$ statistic is inherently two-sided.

23.6 Unknown \(\sigma^2\): \(t_1\)-test and \(F_{1,1}\)-test

If $\sigma^2$ is unknown, we cannot use $Z_1/\sigma$. But under both $H_0$ and $H_1$, $$Z_2 \sim N(0,\sigma^2),$$ so the residual coordinate provides a variance estimate.

$$T = \frac{Z_1}{|Z_2|} = \frac{Z_1/\sigma}{\sqrt{(Z_2^2/\sigma^2)/1}} \sim t_1,$$ $$T^2 = \frac{Z_1^2}{Z_2^2} \sim F_{1,1}.$$
Signal/noise is the right intuition, but mathematically the denominator is an estimated variance scale built from a chi-squared residual term. It is not “difference between $Z_1$ and $Z_2$”; it is the relative dominance of signal over residual magnitude.

23.7 Geometric meaning: why \(\cot \theta\) appears

Let $\theta$ be the angle between the observed vector $Z$ and the $Z_1$-axis. Then $$Z_1 = \|Z\| \cos \theta, \qquad Z_2 = \|Z\| \sin \theta,$$ so $$\frac{Z_1}{Z_2} = \cot \theta.$$

The statistic $|Z_1|/|Z_2| = |\cot \theta|$ measures alignment with the signal axis. Small angle means the point hugs the horizontal axis, so signal dominates residual noise.
Compare $(1,2)$ and $(4,2)$. The vertical coordinate is the same, but the second point is more aligned with the signal axis because the horizontal coordinate is larger. That is exactly how the alternative changes the geometry.

The One-Sample \(t\)-Test Is Canonical in Disguise

23.8 The \(n=2\) rotation

Now take the familiar one-sample model with two observations: $$X_1, X_2 \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(\mu,\sigma^2).$$ The mean vector is $\mu(1,1)^T$, so the signal direction is the line $X_1 = X_2$.

Use the orthogonal matrix $$Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z = Q^T X = \begin{pmatrix} (X_1 + X_2)/\sqrt 2 \\ (X_2 - X_1)/\sqrt 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$ Then $$Z \sim N_2 \left( \binom{\sqrt 2\,\mu}{0}, \sigma^2 I_2 \right).$$
Orthogonal transformations preserve the spherical noise covariance, but they also rotate the mean vector. We choose $Q$ so the mean lands entirely in the first coordinate and the second coordinate becomes pure residual noise.

So the usual one-sample test is literally the canonical model in a rotated coordinate system: $$Z_1 = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad Z_2 = \frac{X_2 - X_1}{\sqrt 2}.$$

23.9 Back-translation to the classical statistic

In the rotated basis, the lecture-23 statistic is $$\frac{Z_1}{|Z_2|} = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{|X_1 - X_2|}.$$ But for $n=2$, the sample mean and sample standard deviation satisfy $$\bar X = \frac{X_1 + X_2}{2}, \qquad S = \frac{|X_1 - X_2|}{\sqrt 2},$$ so $$\frac{Z_1}{|Z_2|} = \frac{\sqrt 2\,\bar X}{S}.$$

The classical one-sample $t$-statistic is the ratio of the projection onto the signal direction $(1,1)$ to the normalized projection onto the orthogonal residual direction $(-1,1)$.

General One-Sample \(t\)-Test and Residual Geometry

23.10 Signal direction and residual subspace

For $X_1,\ldots,X_n \stackrel{iid}{\sim} N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, write $$X = (X_1,\ldots,X_n)^T \sim N_n(\mu \mathbf{1}, \sigma^2 I_n),$$ where $\mathbf{1}=(1,\ldots,1)^T$.

$$\bar X = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i, \qquad S^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2, \qquad S = \sqrt{S^2}.$$

Let $\mathbf q_1 = \mathbf 1/\sqrt n$ and choose $Q_r$ whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{span}(\mathbf 1)$. Then $$Q = [\mathbf q_1 \mid Q_r]$$ is orthogonal, and the rotated coordinates are $$Z_1 = \mathbf q_1^T X = \sqrt n\,\bar X, \qquad Z_r = Q_r^T X \in \mathbb R^{n-1}.$$

In this basis, $$\binom{Z_1}{Z_r} \sim N_n \left( \binom{\sqrt n\,\mu}{0}, \sigma^2 I_n \right).$$ So the signal lives in one coordinate and the residual part lives in an $(n-1)$-dimensional orthogonal subspace.

23.11 Why \((n-1)S^2/\sigma^2 \sim \chi^2_{n-1}\)

Orthogonal decomposition gives $$\|X\|^2 = Z_1^2 + \|Z_r\|^2,$$ and rearranging shows $$\|Z_r\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \bar X)^2 = (n-1)S^2.$$

Since $Z_r \sim N_{n-1}(0,\sigma^2 I_{n-1})$, $$\frac{\|Z_r\|^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}.$$ Equivalently, $$\frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}.$$ Also, $Z_1$ and $Z_r$ are independent, so $\bar X$ and $S^2$ are independent.
Subtracting the mean is what makes these coordinates residuals. The chi-squared comes from the squared length of the residual vector after rotating into an orthonormal residual basis, not from centering alone.
The number $n-1$ is the residual dimension: after fitting one mean direction, only $n-1$ independent noise directions remain.

23.12 The classical \(t\) and \(F\) forms

If the null hypothesis is $H_0:\mu=\mu_0$, the same geometry applies after centering the data by $\mu_0$. Under $H_0$, $$\frac{\sqrt n\,(\bar X - \mu_0)}{\sigma} \sim N(0,1).$$ Replacing $\sigma$ by the residual estimate $S$ gives the usual statistic.

$$T = \frac{\sqrt n\,(\bar X - \mu_0)}{S} = \frac{Z_1/\sigma}{\sqrt{(\|Z_r\|^2/\sigma^2)/(n-1)}} \sim t_{n-1},$$ $$T^2 \sim F_{1,n-1}.$$
The denominator is $$\frac{\|Z_r\|^2}{n-1},$$ which is average squared residual variation per residual direction. This is exactly the abstract $V/d$ term in the definition of the $t$-distribution.

23.13 Nuisance directions preview

A direction whose mean is unrestricted under both $H_0$ and $H_1$ is neither tested signal nor usable residual noise. It carries no information about the null-versus-alternative comparison, so the canonical linear model simply drops it.

Master Summary and Formula Sheet

Lecture 23 core formulas

$$\chi^2_d = \sum_{j=1}^d U_j^2, \qquad t_d = \frac{Z}{\sqrt{V/d}}, \qquad F_{d_1,d_2} = \frac{V_1/d_1}{V_2/d_2}, \qquad t_d^2 \sim F_{1,d}.$$
$$Z \sim N_n(0,\sigma^2 I_n), \quad V \subseteq \mathbb R^n,\ \dim(V)=d \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \|P_V Z\|^2 \sim \sigma^2 \chi^2_d.$$
$$Z_1 = \sqrt n\,\bar X, \qquad \|Z_r\|^2 = (n-1)S^2, \qquad \frac{(n-1)S^2}{\sigma^2} \sim \chi^2_{n-1}, \qquad \frac{\sqrt n\,(\bar X-\mu_0)}{S} \sim t_{n-1} \text{ under } H_0.$$

What to remember conceptually

Every test in this lecture is a ratio of projection magnitudes onto orthogonal subspaces. The only moving parts are:
  • Which subspace contains the signal?
  • Which orthogonal subspace contains pure residual noise?
  • How many dimensions does each subspace have?
  • Is the variance known or estimated from the residual piece?

Signal/noise is the right story to keep in mind, but the exact mathematics is: numerator = Gaussian projection in the tested direction, denominator = square root of an independent chi-squared-per-degree-of-freedom term built from the residual projection.

Next step: the full canonical linear model, where the tested signal block can have dimension greater than 1 and nuisance directions can be present.

Common Mistakes

  • Thinking an orthogonal transformation leaves the mean unchanged. It preserves the covariance shape but rotates the mean vector too.
  • Thinking the alternative must change the residual coordinate $Z_2$. It does not; the evidence comes from $Z_1$ becoming large relative to $Z_2$.
  • Thinking the statistic measures the difference between signal and noise. It measures a ratio: signal magnitude divided by estimated noise scale.
  • Thinking a chi-squared appears just because a variable is centered at 0. The real reason is a sum of squares of independent standardized Gaussian residual coordinates.
  • Thinking the $d$ in $\chi^2_d$ or $V/d$ is arbitrary. It is the dimension of the relevant residual subspace, i.e. the degrees of freedom.
  • Forgetting that the squared $z$-test and $\chi^2_1$-test are the same two-sided test, and that squaring destroys one-sided sign information.
